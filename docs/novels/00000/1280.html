The glass doors of the Penhaligon Media boardroom shuddered as they slammed shut, the sound echoing like a gunshot through the silence of the executive suite. Jada Penhaligon stood behind her mahogany desk, her fingers white-knuckled as she clutched a stack of legal filings that signaled the end of her family’s three-generation reign. Across from her, Soren Sterling-Ross moved with the predatory grace of a man who owned the air in the room. He didn’t sit. He paced, his tailored charcoal suit jacket discarded over the back of a leather chair, his white shirt sleeves rolled to his elbows, revealing the sharp, familiar line of his forearms.

"You didn't just buy the shares, Soren," Jada said, her voice steady despite the adrenaline spiking in her veins. "You dismantled a legacy. My grandfather built this network on integrity, not on hostile takeovers orchestrated by a man who once swore he wanted to protect it."

Soren stopped his restless movement. He leaned against the floor-to-ceiling glass wall, the city lights of Manhattan glittering behind him like a shattered kaleidoscope. His gaze locked onto hers, heavy and unrelenting. "Integrity was a luxury this company couldn't afford, Jada. The quarterly earnings were hemorrhaging. You were drowning in debt, blind to the fact that your board was sharpening the knives behind your back. I didn't orchestrate a downfall; I performed an extraction."

"An extraction?" Jada laughed, a sharp, humorless sound. "You’ve spent the last six hours auditing our digital archives, interrogating my department heads, and questioning every creative decision I’ve made in the last year. That isn't an extraction. That’s a siege."

He pushed off the glass and took a step toward the desk, though he stopped well short of crossing the invisible boundary between them. The space between them felt charged, a vacuum that pulled at the oxygen. "If I hadn't stepped in, you would have been ousted by a private equity firm that would have gutted the archives and sold the real estate by Tuesday. I kept the soul of the company intact. I just happen to be the one holding the leash now."

Jada rounded the desk, needing to close the distance to see if the man she remembered was still buried beneath the cold, calculating veneer of the billionaire investor. "You think holding the leash makes you the master? This is my office. My name is on the masthead. I won't let you turn my vision into a subsidiary of your ego."

"My ego isn't the problem," Soren countered, his voice dropping to a low, gravelly register that made her breath hitch. "The problem is that you still think we’re fighting for the same thing when we’ve been playing different games for years. Do you have any idea how many nights I spent staring at the ceiling, wondering if you’d finally realize that your obsession with this company was the third person in our marriage?"

The air turned thick, heavy with the ghosts of the past. Jada straightened her posture, refusing to blink. "We don't get to talk about the marriage, Soren. That ended when you chose your portfolio over our anniversary dinner two years ago. This is about business. If you want to audit me, fine. Let’s look at the numbers. Let’s look at why you’re actually here."

Soren’s jaw tightened. He walked to the window again, looking down at the crawling traffic below. The tension in his shoulders was palpable. He was a man who thrived on control, yet he seemed entirely unmoored by her presence. "I’m here because the numbers stopped making sense the moment I lost you. Every acquisition I made, every merger I finalized—they all felt like empty victories. I built a mountain of assets just to see if I could find a way back to a conversation where you weren't looking at me like a competitor."

"Then you’re a fool," Jada whispered. "A hostile takeover is a hell of a way to win a conversation."

"It was the only way to get you to stop running," he replied, turning back to face her. His eyes were dark, searching her face with an intensity that felt like a physical weight. "I needed to force the situation. I needed you to stand your ground, to be the woman who refused to fold under pressure. I needed to see if the fire was still there."

"The fire is there, Soren," she said, her voice rising with a controlled, simmering fury. "But it’s burning the wrong people. You think you’ve won the keys to the kingdom? You’ve just walked into a hornet's nest. I have legal counsel arriving in an hour. We’re going to challenge the legitimacy of your acquisition on the grounds of undisclosed conflicts of interest."

Soren stepped forward again, this time closing the gap until he was hovering just outside her personal space. He didn't touch her, but the heat radiating from him was unmistakable. "Let them come. I’ve already anticipated every move your legal team will make. You’re playing checkers, Jada. I’ve been playing chess for a decade."

"Then let’s see how you handle a checkmate," she retorted, turning on her heel to walk toward the door. She didn't look back, even as she felt his eyes tracking her every movement.

The hallway leading to the archives was dimly lit, the fluorescent tubes flickering with a hum that grated on Jada’s nerves. She needed to find the original ledger from the 2022 fiscal audit—the document that proved Soren had been secretly buying shares long before the official announcement. If she could prove he’d been positioning himself while they were still legally married, the ethics board would have a field day.

She swiped her keycard, the door clicking open to reveal rows of filing cabinets that smelled of dust and aged paper. She began pulling files, her heart hammering against her ribs.

"You won't find what you're looking for in the physical files," a voice said from the shadows near the back wall.

Jada jumped, dropping a heavy folder. Soren emerged from behind a row of shelves, his sleeves still rolled up, his tie loosened. He looked exhausted, yet there was a flicker of something raw in his expression. "I moved them to the secure vault yesterday. The one you lost the access code to when you changed the security protocols in June."

"You changed the codes?" Jada asked, her voice trembling with indignation. "How did you even have the authorization? The board didn't vote on security clearance changes."

"I am the board, Jada," he said, walking toward her. He stopped a few feet away, his gaze sweeping over the scattered papers on the floor. "And I did it to keep you from digging into things that were never meant to be seen. You think I’m trying to ruin you, but I’m trying to save you from your own desperation. You’re working yourself to the bone trying to uphold a legacy that wasn't built to survive the digital age. Your grandfather was a genius, but he wasn't a prophet."

"Don't you dare speak about him as if you understand his vision," she snapped, stepping toward him. "He understood that people matter more than profit margins. He knew that if you treat your employees like commodities, you lose the heart of the business."

"We’ve spent so much time building barriers that we’ve forgotten how to stand in the same room without looking for an exit strategy. Tonight, the board is meeting at the rooftop bar. "I want you to admit that we’re better when we aren't trying to outmaneuver one another," he replied. "Meet me on the roof in twenty minutes. If you’re not there, I’ll assume you’ve chosen the fight. He turned and walked toward the door, leaving her alone in the dusty, quiet silence of the archives. Jada looked at the piles of paper, the remnants of a history she was terrified to lose, and realized that for the first time in years, the company didn't feel like a fortress. It felt like a cage.

The sky was a deep, bruised purple, the stars obscured by the city lights. Jada walked toward the edge of the terrace, her heels clicking against the stone tiles. Soren stood by the parapet, his back to the door, his hands clasped behind him. He looked like a statue carved from granite, unmoving against the wind.

He didn't turn when she approached. He stayed focused on the horizon, his silhouette stark against the glow of the skyline. "I didn't think you’d come."

"I’m here for the company, not for you," Jada said, though she stood close enough to feel the chill emanating from the stone where he had been leaning.

Soren turned, his eyes searching hers. The wind caught his dark hair, pulling it across his forehead, but he didn't brush it away. "The company is part of us. It always has been. Even when we were at our worst, we were arguing about this place. We defined ourselves by what we could build."

"We defined ourselves by what we could conquer," Jada corrected. "There's a difference."

"I’m tired of conquering," he said, his voice barely audible over the wind. "I have the shares. I have the leverage. I could ruin you by morning, and you know it. But I’m standing here, asking you to take them back."

He pulled a document from his jacket pocket—a legal transfer of majority interest. He held it out, not as a bribe, but as a burden.
